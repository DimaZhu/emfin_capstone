The short version
1. The engine is careful and honest. Every candidate is checked on fresh simulated futures it
never saw, under four different market assumption sets, and failures stay visible instead of being
tuned away.
2. At your confirmed $250k/month, the safe answer is boring. Every objective formula lands on
roughly the same mix: about 76% cash, 9% Treasuries, 5% US stocks, 10% property. The risk limits
make that choice, not the math of the objective.
3. Clever has not beaten simple. Across 19 years of real history and thousands of simulated
futures, no utility formula, trading rule or resampling scheme has a proven edge. Simple fixed mixes
pass every check that the learned trading rules fail.
4. Rebalance rarely. Annual rebalancing scores best of the four frequencies tested at 1, 3 and 5
years. But the margin is small, and past one year no frequency passes all safety checks, so "annual"
is the best of what was tested, not a proven optimum.
5. "Passed" never meant "every payment guaranteed". The setup knowingly tolerates a small
shortfall risk on each payment. A stricter 99% all-payments check now exists and is reported
separately.
6. The real open risks are the market model, not the objectives. Markets cluster calm and stormy
weeks; the default model does not. And the honest margin for "the model itself is wrong" comes out
larger than the whole balance sheet. Both are documented, neither is hidden.
7. The 76% cash is really "76% in T-bills". The model's cash earns a placeholder 0%, and the only
bond fund it can buy lost 18% in its worst real year. With a realistic 4% on the cash, the score rises
$3.6M and the bond fund disappears. A real deposit or bill asset is the highest-value open fix.
8. The model survived an inside-out audit. Every module was re-read, eleven independent
numerical tests match the engine to hand-computed answers, and every modeling dial was
perturbed one at a time. No errors found; the validated answer sits in a wide, flat safe region (usually
69-78% cash across fresh seeds) rather than at a tuned point, and the market model - not the math remains the real risk.

Two terms used throughout: the score of a plan is its certainty equivalent: the guaranteed dollar amount,

Higher is better. A market law is one complete set of assumptions about how prices move; final checks run
under four different laws so that no answer depends on a single guess about the future.

The six parts. Data in, verdict out. The optimizer never sees the paths it is judged on.

- Inputs. Wealth, the payment calendar, risk limits and current holdings, including the property that stays
unsold.

- Estimation. Real weekly total-return histories for the four funds (2005-2026), the quarterly URA property
index and the Fed's SGD/USD series. Averages and correlations are shrunk toward safe values because
raw estimates are noisy.
- Scenarios. Thousands of simulated 52-week futures for all assets at once, including occasional bad
weeks (a two-state Gaussian mixture, 90/10 probability, variance 0.75x/3.25x).
- Optimizer. Picks the long-only mix with the best score subject to every funding and loss limit, using
cumulative provisioning for the payments.
- Independent judge. The frozen mix is re-checked on entirely fresh paths, under four market laws,
against every limit: each payment, final loss, worst-path loss, holdings. A fail is reported, never repaired
by retuning.
- Execution audit. A separate cash-first ledger simulates actually paying: cash first, then proportional
sales, with arrears tracked. It answers the only question that matters in practice: was every payment
actually made?

3. Does the market model match reality?
Your copy of Meucci's Risk and Asset Allocation (2005) was used as the audit standard. The audit fixed four
concrete errors in the engine (an interpolated-quantile bug, a crash with a single stochastic asset, stale
documentation, and an overclaimed formula). More importantly, it exposed that the basic checks the book
demands before assuming independent weeks had never been run. This section is those checks.
Weekly returns have no memory of direction, but volatility has memory. Last week's return tells you
almost nothing about this week's direction (correlations between -0.07 and +0.02). But last week's squared
return does predict this week's squared return (correlations up to 0.34): calm weeks follow calm weeks,
storms follow storms. The default law treats every week as independent, so it cannot produce clusters of
bad weeks.

Correlation of each series with its own previous week, 2005-2026 (1,129 weeks). Left bars: returns. Right bars: squared returns.
The tall right bars are volatility clustering, which the default model omits.

- Equities are also left-skewed and fat-tailed in the data (VTI sample skewness -0.99, excess kurtosis 9.1).
The default mixture is symmetric with excess kurtosis 1.69: milder tails than history.
- A history-bootstrap variant (EWMA-FHS) reduces the clustering in its residuals to about 0.10-0.12, but
that is not zero and not proof it is better.
- These are descriptive facts about 21 years, not guarantees about the next one.

4. The forecast-model contest
If the default law misses volatility clustering, would a conditional-volatility model do better? Four models
were committed in advance, fit on the past only, and scored on one-week-ahead distribution forecasts for
2019-2025. The selection was locked on 2014-2018 data before the final scoring. This is a forecasting
contest, not a portfolio result.

- The GARCH-variance model beats the current one on the final period (error 0.6633 vs 0.6756), in each

- But in the 2008 stress slice every model was caught out far more than its promised 5%: current model
21.5%, GARCH 13.9%, history bootstrap 8.9%. No candidate handled a real crisis as advertised.
- A mathematical catch blocks ordinary GARCH for annual paths: its unbounded recursion can produce
infinite price moments beyond a few weeks. A capped variant (volatility limited to 3x baseline) was built
to fix exactly this, and its finite moments are proved.
- The capped model improves weekly forecasts by about 1.4%, but on full-year evidence it shows no
proven benefit: 52-week forecasts score 0.20% better, 26-week worse, and the judge accepted no
historical blend of it. It stays research-only; you have not approved promoting it.

8. How wrong could the model itself be?
On top of simulating market risk, the engine can charge each portfolio a safety margin for model risk:
pretend the fitted law could be off by some radius, and price the worst case inside it (a Wasserstein, or W1,
margin). This is where the most uncomfortable finding of the whole project sits.

Ballast funding-risk engine - unified scientific report - 8 November 2026, 3rd ed.

Private research, not investment advice - page 9

The assumed model-wrongness margin implies a $40k buffer. The margin actually measured from 21 years of history implies
$813M: more than eight times the balance sheet, against a per-payment budget of $50k. Log scale.

- The working radius (0.00002) was always a placeholder. Measured from 21 complete years of history,
year-to-year model drift implies a radius of about 0.41 (range 0.39-0.63 across 2013-2024).
- At that honest radius, the unavoidable safety buffer is about $813M on a $100M balance sheet: no
long-only portfolio can be certified against a $50k per-payment budget. The calibration therefore returns
no candidate at all.
- Nothing was tuned to hide this: the placeholder stays, labelled uncalibrated, and the conflict is shown in
the dashboard. Shrinking the radius until something passes would be exactly the kind of quiet repair this
project refuses to do.
- Part of the gap is a crude metric: the current measure gives no credit for diversification and allows
economically impossible perturbed scenarios. Redesigning it is an open research item, not a number to
quietly adjust.

9. Can month-by-month trading beat a fixed plan?
You asked for a mathematical optimization, not a bucket playbook. So a feedback rule was trained: eight
learned parameters that map only what is knowable at the time (current wealth, remaining payments,
drawdown, recent returns) into target weights, with realistic frictions (0.05% per trade, one-week
settlement). Selection was frozen before final judging; simple fixed mixes were compared on identical paths
and costs.

Scores after costs. The learned feedback rule beats the original static mix, but a rule that makes the same first trade and then
does nothing beats the feedback rule. Both learned variants fail when expected returns are 2pp lower; the simple static mixes
pass all four laws.

- On the two laws it was designed for, the feedback rule beats the static start by about $0.56-0.59M on a
~$98M score, after roughly $9k of fees.
- Attribution shows all of that gain is the first trade (a better starting mix). The month-to-month reactions
themselves subtract about $20-28k relative to doing nothing after day one.
- The feedback rule and its first-trade-only variant both fail the two lower-return laws. Three simple
fixed-mix baselines pass all four laws.
- A robust redesign (trained to do well under the worst of all four laws) found no acceptable rule: 360
candidates evaluated, none of the top 12 passed validation everywhere.
- How far from optimal is anything? A cheating bound (a clairvoyant with no costs reaches a $197-241M
score vs the ~$98M achieved) is far too loose to say. There is no near-optimality certificate, and no
proven better sell rule. The search failing does not prove a good rule cannot exist.

11. Why is so much in cash?
You asked this directly: why does the answer hold 76% cash, when a Treasury bond is low-risk and pays a
decent yield, especially over one year? Fair question, and the model has a precise answer in three parts.
1. The model has no Treasury bill. The only bond it can buy is IEF, a fund holding 7-10 year Treasuries. A
one-year bill held to the end is nearly riskless: you know exactly what you get back. A 7-10 year bond fund
is different: when interest rates rise its price falls, and no maturity date pulls it back inside your one-year
window. In the fitted model the fund carries 6.4% yearly risk for about 3.5-4% expected return; in real history
its worst year was -17.9% (in 2022, the year rates jumped, it lost 15.2%). Over a one-year horizon the
model is right to treat it as a risky asset, not as a safe 5%.
2. The 76% is forced by the safety rules, not chosen for its return. Left to itself, the optimizer picks only
35.5% cash and scores $100.1M, but that mix fails the held-out path-loss check. The engine then blends
toward a 90%-cash anchor until every check holds, landing at 76%. Passing the checks costs about $1.9M
of score per year. The binding constraints are the twelve payment tests (each $250k payment may lose at
most $50k in its 5% tail, with the family-wide bar tightened for having twelve tests), the 15% worst-path loss
limit, and a fixed model-miss buffer. The model's cash earns a placeholder 0%, so it is the one asset that
can never fail any of these checks.
3. Read "cash" as "T-bills" and the puzzle flips. Rerun with the cash earning a real deposit rate: at 2%
the score rises $2.1M; at 4% (roughly a current bill yield) it rises $3.6M, and the model drops the Treasury
fund entirely - why hold a 3.6% fund with real price risk when bills pay 4% with none? So the practical
reading is not "76% idle" but "76% in bills or money-market at about 4-5%, because the rules demand
something that cannot fall, and the only bond fund on offer can fall".

much safer than stocks but far from riskless over one year. Right: giving the cash a real yield raises the score and pushes the

Making the cash realistic is the single most valuable open fix measured in this report: worth more than every

13. The portfolio at $50k, $100k and $250k a month
What does the safe portfolio actually look like at each payout level? Section 12 traced the score across
payout sizes; this section lays out the validated portfolio itself at $50k, $100k and $250k a month, with
$125k added because it sits exactly at the engine's refusal boundary.
Why the engine refuses below $200k a month, in plain words. Two safety settings collide there. The
first is a fixed model-miss buffer: every payment must survive an extra margin for the chance the market
model itself is wrong, and that margin is a fixed $40k per payment. The second is a per-payment allowance:
the tail-risk budget for each payment is 20% of that payment. At $250k a month the allowance is $50k and
comfortably covers the $40k buffer. At $125k the allowance is $25k - smaller than the buffer - and the
settings contradict each other. The engine then declines to run instead of silently weakening either setting.
That is a guardrail doing its job, not a claim that smaller payouts are infeasible.
A clearly labeled sensitivity, not a changed default. To show the portfolios anyway, one number is
scaled: the model-miss buffer is shrunk in proportion to the payment, keeping it at 80% of the allowance the same ratio it has at the confirmed $250k level. No budget, limit or check is loosened; every safety test
still runs and still passes with margin. The default configuration is untouched, and the refusal itself is
reported above, not hidden.

15. "Passed" does not mean "paid"
This distinction caused real confusion during the project, so it gets its own section. The declared funding
limit allows each payment a 20% tail-shortfall allowance at 95% confidence. That is not a promise to
make every payment. Counterfactual stress fixtures make the gap concrete:

A deliberately extreme $95M final call: the share of validation paths paying every call collapses as the mix is made safer, because
the obligation is simply too large. Even 90% cash misses $5M on every path. Not your plan; a stress test.

- With a $60M early call plus a $30M final call, a validation-calibrated portfolio passes every declared
model limit while making every payment on only about 84% of paths.
- With your actual $3M schedule the risk is remote (the cash alone covers years of payments), which is
exactly why the passing label is weak evidence there.
- Two fixes are now built in: a strict 99% all-payments check (at least 99% of fresh paths must make
every payment, with an exact binomial lower bound) and a separate execution audit that counts actual
unpaid calls in the cash-first ledger.
- Sample honesty: proving a 99% all-paid rate with zero observed misses takes at least 299 independent
paths; the studies use 1,600.
- Both verdicts are shown side by side, and the strict check and the all-risk check can disagree. When
they do, both are reported.

18. What outside research and practice say
Thirteen sources (academic papers and family-office practice) were fetched and read. The short version:
the engine's core frame is the endorsed one, and practice adds a liquidity playbook on top of it.
- Secure the liability first, take risk with what is left. The surplus/LDI literature (Sharpe/Scott/Watson
2008; Ashton 2011) endorses exactly the engine's frame: optimize wealth after obligations, not raw
returns.
- Selling at a bad time has a name: sequence-of-returns risk. With withdrawals, the order of returns
matters more than the average (Pfau). Selling a bit of everything every month is the textbook thing to
avoid.
- A cash reserve of 1-2 years of payouts, refilled only from winners and never by selling what is down,
improved 30-year plan survival by up to 6 points in one study (Pfeiffer/Salter/Evensky 2013). For you that
is $3-6M, cheap insurance at a 3% payout.
- But buckets are mostly relabeling: a cash bucket is financially near-equivalent to a static mix with the
same overall allocation (Tomlinson 2020). The benefit is behavior and costs, not magic return.
- Rebalance with tolerance bands (~20% of target weight), not a calendar (Kitces 2015; Daryanani
2007). In a drawdown, fund spending by selling the most overweight or most appreciated asset first.
- Family offices typically hold 0-2 (up to 3) years of spending in cash or near-cash, and use a post-stress
liquidity ratio of 3x annual needs as a safety gauge (Cambridge Associates 2023; RBC/Campden 2025).
- Property crowds out equity in optimal household portfolios (Cocco 2005): the fixed 10% property
correctly pushes the liquid sleeve more conservative.
Where this meets the engine's results: the annual-rebalancing win and the cash-heavy validated mix agree
with practice. The open tension is bands versus calendar: the engine tested calendar schedules only, and
the literature suggests bands are the better rule. The liquidity-tier suggestion is the literature brief author's
judgment, flagged as judgment, not engine evidence.

20. The verdict
On the confirmed plan, the defensible answer is a boring one. A cash-heavy mix (about 76% cash, 9%
Treasuries, 5% US stocks, 10% property) passes every check under every market law tested, funds every
payment on all 1,600 fresh paths, and no cleverer alternative proved itself better. At a 3% annual payout,
safety is cheap and the payout is the tail risk that matters least; the model is the tail risk that matters most.
- Objective: keep CARA-on-surplus. The four-way comparison shows the choice does not drive the
answer at these limits.
- Trading: one good allocation, then cash-first. Learned monthly feedback adds nothing after its first trade
and breaks when returns disappoint; no robust rule was found.
- Rebalancing: annual is the best of the four tested, at every horizon, on score. At one year it also passes
every check. Past one year, nothing passes everything, so treat annual as "best tested", not "proven".
- Safety labels: read the strict 99% all-payments line and the execution audit when asking "will every
payment be made?", not the 20% allowance line.
- Biggest open risks: crisis tails in the market model and the uncalibrated model-wrongness margin, with
volatility clustering real but shown not to understate this plan at one year (section 17). All are
documented; none was tuned away.
What would change the answer: a real deposit or T-bill asset for the cash (worth about +$3.6M a year in
the model, section 11); promoting the capped conditional-volatility model after a real annual validation (your
decision, currently unapproved); actual property, rent, settlement and cost data; a redesigned, calibrated
model-risk margin; tolerance-band rebalancing tests; and real client constraints beyond the monthly
amount.
What the overnight audit changed: the evidence, not the answer. A full re-read of the engine, eleven
independent numerical tests, nineteen one-decision perturbations and direct weekly and yearly tail checks
against realized history found no errors and no hidden load-bearing choice. The crash-mixture law is now
backed by data rather than judgment alone, and the one soft spot the audit surfaced - the flat blend
optimum (62-90% cash across seeds, usually 69-78%) - is reported openly in section 17.
What this report is not: investment advice, a client calibration, or a proof about the future. It is a record of
what fourteen careful experiments and a full inside-out audit found, and of exactly where they stop.
